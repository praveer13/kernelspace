# T7.L1 — The Objective Function: Goodput, SLOs, and Honest Metrics

_Track T7: Economics & SLO Engineering · ~20 min · kernelspace_

> Every previous track optimized something. This track defines the thing. One number the business understands, one curve the engineers argue with, and the reason "10,000 tok/s" on a benchmark page means nothing.
T5.L3 defined the metrics; this track operationalizes them. The serving business has exactly one objective function: **goodput — requests per second (or tokens per second) delivered within SLO**, which you divide by cost (per GPU-hour) to compare fleets. Not throughput. Not utilization. Requests that meet their latency contract. Everything in T7 is this function's derivatives.

Why the distinction is not pedantry: as batch grows, throughput rises and then flattens against the compute and bandwidth roofs, while latency keeps rising — bigger batches stretch every step and deepen the queue, and past the knee the p99 goes vertical. Completions crawl even as the batch gets enormous. Raw "tok/s" benchmarks are taken *at* the vertical part, where every user has already left. Goodput forces the honest question: at what concurrency does the system stop meeting TTFT/TPOT — and what does that point cost?

---

## The metrics, hardened

- **TTFT** (arrival → first token): queue + prefill (+ KV transfer when disaggregated). The "is it alive" signal. Production SLOs: Meta's app targets **<350 ms**; typical chat SLOs 0.5–2 s depending on prompt class.
- **TPOT/ITL** (per-token decode period): bandwidth ÷ (weight+KV bytes), divided by batch efficiency. The "is it fast" signal. Meta targets **<25 ms TTIT**; chat comfort is <50–80 ms.
- **E2E latency** and its percentiles: what the user feels; **p50 for design, p95/p99 for contracts**. Never one number.
- **Goodput**: throughput *subject to* the above — e.g. req/s with TTFT < 2 s AND TPOT < 100 ms. Capacity planning's only honest input.
- **Interactivity** (tok/s/user) vs **throughput** (tok/s/GPU): the two ends of the dial T7.L3 turns. Benchmarks that don't name which end they're showing are marketing.

The engine's control loop, one line: **batching puts throughput and latency on a seesaw (bigger batches buy tokens per second and cost TTFT and TPOT), and the SLO picks the operating point.** Your lab-06 scheduler is that sentence made executable — you implemented admission policy against this exact objective function.

---

- **<350 ms** — Meta TTFT SLO (Production app target (Oct 2025 writeup).)
- **<25 ms** — Meta TTIT SLO (Per-token interactivity target — decode loop latency budget.)
- **p95** — the honest percentile (Design at p50, contract at p95. Never one number.)
- **goodput** — the objective (Requests within SLO per second; divide by cost to compare fleets. Everything else is a proxy.)

Your web service degraded gracefully (everyone gets slightly slower); an inference engine has a *cliff* — admission denied is a 429, and memory pressure triggers preemption, so the tail doesn't stretch, it snaps. The metric discipline is identical; the failure geometry is sharper.

## Counting goodput, and the error budget it spends

Counting goodput is mechanical: for every request in a window, check **both** bounds, count the requests that pass both, and divide by the window's length. Take a hypothetical 10 s window with 40 requests, where 31 meet TTFT < 2 s, 34 meet TPOT < 100 ms and 29 meet both. Goodput is 29 ÷ 10 = **2.9 req/s**. Counting only the TTFT bound claims 3.1 req/s, and counting every request claims 4.0.

An SLO is also a budget. A **99% SLO** lets 1 request in 100 miss, and that 1% is the **error budget**: over a hypothetical 1,000,000 requests in a month it is 10,000 misses, spent on deploys, spikes and incidents. What matters day to day is the **burn rate**, the observed miss fraction divided by the budgeted one. If 4% of requests miss against a 1% budget, the burn rate is 4 and a 30-day budget is gone in 30 ÷ 4 = 7.5 days. A burn rate of 1 spends it exactly on schedule. Alert on the burn rate over a window, not on one bad minute.

**Q1. Goodput is defined as…**

- (o1) Throughput within the first-token and inter-token latency targets, the honest capacity metric
- (o2) The fraction of device time spent in useful kernels instead of idle, reported as a utilization percentage
- (o3) Total tokens per second the engine emits across its whole request stream, measured at saturation
- (o4) Requests per second that finish with a success status, regardless of the latency they took

**Q2. A vendor page shows "10,000 tok/s" with no latency numbers. The right reaction is…**

- (o1) Trustworthy once the page names the model, since decode speed follows from the parameter count
- (o2) Uninformative without any latency figures, likely taken well past the knee where goodput collapses
- (o3) Usable after dividing by the chip count, turning it into a figure comparable across vendors
- (o4) Credible for current hardware, since peak tokens per second is the figure capacity plans are built on

**Q3. Throughput and latency sit on a seesaw: tokens per second per GPU rise with load while TTFT and TPOT rise too. The coupling comes from…**

- (o1) Collective communication, whose fixed per-step latency gets added to prefill and to each decode step
- (o2) Quantization, which trades answer quality for speed rather than trading latency for throughput
- (o3) Batching, which grows throughput by amortizing weight reads while queues and decode steps lengthen
- (o4) The tokenizer, whose per-request host time delays the first token and each later token

**Q4. Why design at p50 but contract at p95?**

- (o1) The slowest requests come mostly from client networks and say nothing about the engine, and are excluded
- (o2) The median and the tail move together, and a system that meets one target will also meet the other
- (o3) The median sizes for the typical user while the tail is promised, and one number cannot do both
- (o4) The median is stable with few samples while the tail is too noisy to design with, and noise decides

**Q5. A service has a 99% SLO over 30 days, and 4% of its requests have missed the SLO since the window opened. The budget lasts about…**

- (o1) Ten days: only the 3% above the allowed 1% counts against the budget, so it is used up at three times the pace
- (o2) A hundred and twenty days: a miss rate four times the budget stretches the window by that same factor of four
- (o3) Seven and a half days: 4% missing against a 1% budget is a burn rate of 4, so the 30 days shrink to a quarter
- (o4) Thirty days: the SLO is judged once when the window closes, and a bad stretch can still be paid back by then
