# T7.L5 — Autoscaling & the Planner: Capacity as a Control Loop

_Track T7: Economics & SLO Engineering · ~25 min · kernelspace_

> The last lesson of the course: the component that watches queue depth and TTFT, then adds or removes prefill and decode workers before the SLO breaks. Dynamo calls it the Planner. You have met it before — as the thing the whole course was secretly about.
You've built every piece the loop controls: the admission valve (lab 06), the block manager (lab 02), the intake queue (lab 04), the disaggregated fleets (T6.L3), the economics of the operating point (T7.L3–L4). The Planner closes the loop: **observe** (queue depth, TTFT/TPOT, KV utilization, GPU load) → **decide** (scale prefill? decode? rebalance the split? reroute?) → **act** (add/drain workers, adjust routing weights, shed load).

Why this is hard and not just "HPA on a custom metric": inference capacity is *discrete and slow* (a new worker takes minutes to load 100s of GB of weights — T7.L2's cold start), the workloads are *two different physics* (prefill compute-bound, decode bandwidth-bound — T5.L3), the signals are *leading* (queue depth rises *before* TTFT breaks, so even a planner that targets TTFT and ITL SLAs, as Dynamo's does, has to act on queue and utilization signals or traffic forecasts rather than wait for the measured latency), and mistakes are *asymmetric* (under-provisioning violates SLOs now; over-provisioning violates the margin report monthly — T7.L4).

---

## The Planner's playbook (Dynamo's, and yours)

1. **Signal selection.** Queue depth and *oldest-waiting-age* lead TTFT by the queue time; KV-block utilization leads preemption; TPOT drift leads decode saturation. Measured TTFT is a lagging confirmation. Dynamo's Planner can target TTFT and ITL SLAs directly, and its load and throughput modes act on queue-depth and KV-utilization thresholds and traffic forecasts. Alert and scale on leading signals.
2. **Phase-aware scaling.** In disaggregated fleets (T6.L3), scale the phase that's binding: prefill queue → add prefill workers (compute); decode TPOT creeping → add decode workers (bandwidth+KV); and *rebalance the split* when the traffic mix drifts (summarization week vs chat week). The prefill:decode GPU ratio is a continuous variable, not an architecture.
3. **Cold-start economics.** New workers cost minutes of load time. The mitigations are a lesson stack of their own: keep-warm pools for predictable peaks (diurnal traffic is cron-shaped), fast loaders (safetensors + direct-to-GPU streaming, Rust loaders like modelexpress — T3's language again), weight-sharing when the model is already resident elsewhere (NVLink copy over network pull).
4. **Shed honestly.** When capacity can't arrive in time, this course's ladder, in order, is queue (bounded; TTFT dies as it deepens), then shed (some users fail fast — your lab-06/fleet intake, now production), then degrade (shorter max output, smaller batch classes). The ladder is a design recommendation, not a policy Dynamo's docs prescribe. An honest shed with a retryable 429 beats a silent SLO violation every time — the user experience of a timeout is worse than a clean "busy."

5. **Failure drills.** Capacity failures are the norm (spot reclaim, node death, hot expert, KV thrash). The Planner's real test is behavior *during* them: reroute, rebalance, and how fast goodput recovers. Fleet Week's incident drills are this paragraph made executable.

- **minutes** — worker cold start (Hundreds of GB of weights. Capacity is slow — signals must lead.)
- **queue age** — the leading signal (Oldest-waiting-age rises before TTFT breaks. Scale on the queue, confirm with the latency.)
- **2 ratios** — prefill:decode split, keep-warm pool (The two continuous variables a Planner owns.)
- **3 options** — queue, shed, degrade (When capacity can't arrive: pick on purpose, in advance.)

---

_the Planner ≡ your autoscaler, finally with real stakes_

- **HPA on CPU%** (Add pods when the metric crosses the line; lag kills you at spike.) ≡ **Planner on queue age + KV utilization + TPOT** (Leading signals chosen per phase; scale the binding fleet, not "the service".)
- **cold JVM pool** (Keep-warm for the 9am spike because JIT+load is slow.) ≡ **keep-warm GPU pool + fast loaders** (Minutes of weight-load: capacity arrives late unless pre-warmed.)
- **graceful degradation ladder** (Queue → 429 → reduced feature set, by policy, in advance.) ≡ **queue → honest shed → degrade output classes** (A retryable busy beats a silent SLO breach. Decided before the incident, not during.)

**Q1. Dynamo's Planner can target TTFT and ITL SLAs, yet a planner should act on queue depth and KV utilization before measured TTFT breaks because…**

- (o1) Scaling on latency oscillates, with added workers lowering it and then triggering an immediate scale-down
- (o2) Queue depth and age lead the violation, with first-token latency including queue time and confirming it late
- (o3) First-token latency is dominated by prefill compute, which is constant per prompt and carries no load information
- (o4) Queue metrics are cheaper to collect, whereas latency needs per-request tracing that slows the serving path

**Q2. Phase-aware scaling means…**

- (o1) Adding capacity to the binding phase of prefill or decode, and shifting the split as the traffic mix drifts
- (o2) Scaling prefill and decode workers together at a fixed ratio, keeping the fleet's architecture balanced
- (o3) Swapping decode chips for newer hardware when per-token latency creeps, with decode bandwidth-bound
- (o4) Adding replicas in stages, with a fraction of the target first and a wait for metrics before each next batch

**Q3. Cold start shapes Planner design via…**

- (o1) Keep-warm pools and fast weight loaders for predictable peaks, with minutes of load time forcing early scaling
- (o2) Over-provisioning each phase by a fixed multiple, with scale-up not needed during the day
- (o3) Larger per-worker batches, with each slow-starting worker carrying more requests and fewer workers needed
- (o4) Treating workers as stateless containers, with a new replica serving as soon as its process is up

**Q4. When capacity cannot arrive in time, the honest approach is…**

- (o1) Walking a ladder fixed beforehand, with a bounded queue then a fast retryable shed then degraded output
- (o2) Restarting the saturated workers to clear their queues, with a fresh worker starting without backlog
- (o3) Letting requests queue without bound, with each admitted request eventually served and no request failed
- (o4) Deciding at the moment of overload which requests to drop, with the right choice depending on the incident's cause
